How to Listen to Criticism Without Getting Defensive

You get defensive because your body reacts to criticism before your brain has evaluated it. The tightening, the urge to cut in, the sudden need to explain: all of that fires in the first two seconds. The fix isn't more willpower. It's a short delay between hearing the words and producing a reply.

Here's how the reflex works, and a specific technique to interrupt it.

## What defensiveness actually looks like

Nobody decides to get defensive. It shows up as a physical sequence:

- **Tightening.** Jaw clenches. Shoulders rise. Your stomach drops or your face gets warm.
- **Interrupting.** You start talking before the other person finishes. Often it's "Yeah, but..." or "That's not what happened."
- **Justifying.** You explain the context, the constraints, the reason you did it. You're building a case before you know what the charge is.

Your brain treats criticism as a threat to your standing. It responds like it would to any threat: fast and without nuance. By the time you're justifying, you've stopped listening. You're rehearsing. The problem is that the reflex can't tell the difference between "you're a bad person" and "the report had two errors." Both get the same response.

## The delay technique: notice, breathe, repeat back

You can't stop the tightening. You can catch it and slow what comes next. Use three steps.

- **Notice the physical cue.** Pick your personal tell. Jaw, shoulders, or stomach. When it fires, that's your signal. Naming it silently ("there it is") takes the edge off, because you've shifted from reacting to observing.
- **Take one slow exhale.** Longer than the inhale. It takes three or four seconds and looks like you're thinking, because you are. Keep your mouth closed until it's done.
- **Repeat back the core of what you heard.** One sentence. "So the issue is that I didn't flag the delay until Thursday." Don't agree. Don't disagree. Just confirm you got it.

Step three does the heavy lifting. Your mouth is busy with something neutral, so it can't be busy with a defence. The other person also feels heard, which usually lowers the temperature on their side too.

## "Let me think about that" is a real answer

Many people feel they must respond on the spot. If they don't, they assume they look weak or evasive. So they fire back something half-formed.

That's backwards. A fast reply to criticism is usually the worst reply you'll give all week.

Your manager says: "You've been dominating the client calls and the team feels sidelined."

Instead of: "I'm the one who has the client relationship, someone has to lead."

Say: "I hadn't seen it that way. Let me think about that and come back to you tomorrow."

This isn't a dodge if you do two things. First, say when you'll respond: "by Friday" or "after the weekend." Second, actually respond. A stated delay with a follow-up reads as maturity. An open-ended "I'll think about it" that goes nowhere reads as a brush-off, and people can tell.

Other lines that work:

- "I want to give that a fair answer. Can I take a day?"
- "That's not what I expected to hear. Give me a minute."
- "I need to sit with that before I respond."

Notice none of them agree or disagree. They just buy space.

## Separate the content from the delivery

Once you've paused, you can do the sorting that defensiveness prevents. Criticism has two layers: what was said, and how it was said. People tend to accept or reject both together.

Try this after the conversation, on paper if it helps:

- **Write down the claim.** Strip out the tone, the word choice, the sarcasm. What is the factual or behavioural point? "Meetings I run go over time." "My emails are hard to follow."
- **Ask if any of it is true.** Not "is it entirely fair?" Just "is there a kernel here?" Most criticism has one, even when it's overstated.
- **Set aside the delivery.** A harsh tone doesn't make the point false. A kind tone doesn't make it true either.

Example. A colleague snaps: "You never read the brief before these meetings." That's exaggerated, and the tone stings. But the kernel might be that you skim, and it shows. You can hold both thoughts: "Never" is wrong, and I could prepare better.

Sometimes the criticism is simply wrong, and that's fine. Sorting means you get to reject it on the merits, not because it hurt. If the delivery was truly out of line, that's a separate conversation. ## When you already disagree

The hardest case is criticism you're sure is wrong. The pull to interrupt is strongest here. The same technique applies: breathe, repeat back, then ask a question instead of arguing. "Can you give me an example?" gets you specifics, and specifics either reveal a real issue or expose a weak one. ## Make the pause automatic

Knowing the technique isn't the same as doing it when your face is hot. The tightening is fast, and the only way to beat a reflex is to rehearse the replacement until it's also a reflex.
