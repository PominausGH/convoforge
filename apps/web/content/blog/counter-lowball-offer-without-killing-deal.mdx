How to Counter a Lowball Offer Without Killing the Deal

Counter a lowball offer by stating enthusiasm, naming one specific number, and then saying nothing. Don't apologise, don't explain, and don't fill the gap. That sequence tells them the offer has to move and that you still want the deal.

Most people fail this in one of two ways. They get insulted and push back too hard, or they get nervous and accept. The counter below avoids both.

## The response that signals "move, but I'm still in"

You need two things in the same breath: warmth about the deal, and clarity that the number is wrong. Here is the shape:

*"Thanks for the offer. I'm genuinely excited about this, and I want to make it work. Based on the scope and what this role pays in the market, I'm looking for $92,000. Can we get there?"*

It does four jobs:

- **Enthusiasm first.** It tells them you're not leaving, so they don't have to defend themselves.
- **"Make it work."** It frames the gap as a shared problem, not a fight.
- **One number.** A range hands them the bottom of it. Give a single figure.
- **"Can we get there?"** It's a question, so it invites a reply and doesn't read as an ultimatum.

What you say instead of the usual mistakes: not "That's way below what I expected" (insult), and not "Okay, that sounds fine, maybe a little more?" (acceptance with a whimper).

## Then stop talking

This is the hard part. After you state the number, **stay silent**. Count to ten in your head if you have to.

Silence works better than justification for a plain reason: every reason you give is a target. "I'm asking for $92,000 because my last role paid $85,000 and I have a mortgage" gives them three things to argue with. They'll pick the weakest one, and you'll spend the next five minutes defending it instead of negotiating the number.

Silence also shifts the pressure. The person who speaks next is usually the one who concedes something. If they say "Hmm, that's higher than we planned," that's not a no. It's a pause where they do the work. Don't rescue them with "But I'm flexible!"

If you struggle to hold quiet while your brain scrambles, the habit is trainable. [How to stop planning your response while someone's talking](/blog/stop-planning-response-while-listening) covers the mental moves that make it easier.

If you do need to give a reason, give one, and make it external: "That's in line with what comparable roles are paying." Market data is harder to argue with than your personal circumstances.

## Which number to anchor on

The number isn't just "higher than theirs." Pick it backward from your walk-away point.

- **Set your floor.** The lowest number you'd actually accept and be happy with. Say it's $80,000.
- **Assume they'll split the difference.** They offered $72,000. If you counter at $88,000, the midpoint is $80,000, exactly your floor. You want to land above it, so counter higher.
- **Anchor at $92,000.** A midpoint is now $82,000, and you have room to concede without going under.

Two refinements. First, **use a specific number, not a round one.** $92,000 sounds like you did the maths. $90,000 sounds like you picked a nice figure. Second, **keep it defensible.** Counter wildly above market and you stop being a negotiating partner and become a problem. The goal is the top of the realistic range, not the moon.

Your tone matters as much as the figure. If you tend to come across as either too soft or too sharp, [how to be direct without sounding blunt](/blog/how-to-be-direct-without-sounding-blunt) gives you phrasings that hold the line without raising the temperature.

## What to do if they don't move at all

Sometimes you state your number, sit through the silence, and hear: "This is the best we can do." Don't accept yet, and don't escalate. Work through these in order.

**1. Ask what's driving the limit.** "Help me understand what's fixed here. Is it the budget for the role, or the level?" You're not arguing, you're looking for the real constraint. A fixed salary band is a different problem from a manager who just didn't try.

**2. Move to other terms.** If the base is capped, ask for what isn't: a signing bonus, an earlier review at six months with a defined raise, extra vacation, a title change, remote days, a budget for learning. Try: "If the base can't move, can we agree on a review at six months, tied to specific goals?" Written down, that is worth real money.

**3. Name your position once, plainly.** "I'd love to say yes. At this number it doesn't work for me. If there's any way to close the gap, I'm in." That's honest, it's not a threat, and it leaves the door open.

**4. Know when the other side holds the cards.** If you have no alternatives and they have five other candidates, your approach changes. [How to negotiate when the other side has more leverage](/blog/negotiate-when-the-other-side-has-more-leverage) covers that situation specifically.

Then be ready to walk. A counter only has force if you're willing to hear "no" and go. If you'd accept anything, you were never negotiating.

## The script, end to end

Here is the whole exchange in four lines:

- **You:** "Thanks, I'm excited about this. Based on the scope and market rates, I'm looking for $92,000. Can we get there?"
- **(Silence.)**
- **Them:** "That's higher than we budgeted. We can do $78,000."
- **You:** "I appreciate the move. I'm still at a gap. What would it take to get closer to $88,000?"

Note what you didn't do: you didn't explain, apologise, or take the first bump. You asked a question that makes them solve the problem.
